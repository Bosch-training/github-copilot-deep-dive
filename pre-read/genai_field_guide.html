01

What is Generative AI?

The one distinction that makes everything else click — and ends a lot of hallway debates.

Most AI you've quietly used for years just sorts things: a spam filter deciding "spam" or "not spam," a fraud system flagging a transaction, a triage tool labeling a ticket "bug" or "feature request." Useful, tidy, and about as creative as a filing cabinet. That's a classification model — it picks from categories that already exist and never once surprises you.

Generative AI is a different animal. Instead of sorting, it produces brand-new content — text, images, video, or audio — from a plain description, with no filing cabinet to check first. Ask it for a sprint summary and it doesn't retrieve one that exists somewhere; it writes one, word by word, on the spot — like a colleague who's very fast, very fluent, and occasionally very confidently wrong. GitHub Copilot Chat lives entirely in this second branch.

Classification

Sorts existing information into known buckets.
"Is this ticket a bug or a feature request?"

Generative

Creates new content that didn't exist a moment ago.
"Write this sprint's status update."

Here's the distinction that actually matters day to day — not "what can GenAI do" in the abstract, but which specific asks play to its strength versus set it up to fail:

✓ Good fit

"Turn these 14 retro sticky notes into three recurring themes."
Spotting patterns across text you provide.

✓ Good fit

"Draft a first-pass status update from this sprint export."
A fast draft you'll still edit and check.

✕ Poor fit

"What was our exact defect count last quarter?"
A factual lookup it has no verified record of.

Takeaway

GenAI is a drafting tool, not a database — treat it like an eager intern with excellent grammar, not an oracle. It's genuinely good at summarizing, drafting, and spotting patterns in text you give it, and genuinely bad at being a source of truth for facts or figures it was never handed in the first place.

02

How does a language model predict text?

Try it yourself below — no math, no dread required.

The "brain" behind Copilot Chat is a large language model (LLM). At some point in the past, it was trained by reading a huge amount of existing text — articles, documentation, code, conversations — and noticing which words tend to follow which other words. That training happened once, ahead of time.

Every time you chat with it afterward, it isn't looking anything up and it isn't learning from you in that moment either. It's doing one thing, over and over: given everything written so far, what's the most likely next word? It writes that word, then asks the same question again for the word after it — many times per response.

Real-world intuition — a rhyme you already know

Say the words "Johnny Johnny…" to almost anyone over the age of five and the reply comes back before they've had time to think: "Yes, papa." Follow with "Eating sugar?" and "No, papa" fires back just as fast, wide-eyed innocence included. Nobody is looking that up mid-conversation — the pattern has been heard so many times that the next words practically finish themselves. That instant, pattern-based completion, filling in what usually comes next based purely on repetition you've absorbed, is a surprisingly good everyday stand-in for what an LLM does — just across the whole of ordinary language, at a vastly larger scale than one memorized rhyme.

The rhyme also shows where the analogy hits its limit, and it's a useful one. Because you know the exact rhyme, your next word is basically a sure thing. An LLM is almost never that certain — it's quietly scoring a handful of plausible next words and picking the strongest one, over and over, without the benefit of a rhyme it's recited a thousand times before. That's exactly what the simulator below lets you do by hand, one word at a time — congratulations, you're about to do an LLM's job at 1/100,000,000th the speed.

This "guess the next word" process is powered by a pattern-recognition design called a transformer — it's what makes the model good at spotting patterns across long stretches of text. Every reply Copilot Chat writes is really thousands of small "Johnny Johnny" moments back to back: given what's been said so far, what's the strongest completion? You don't need the internals; you need the one consequence this produces, which is the entire subject of the next chapter.

03

Why AI sometimes gets it wrong

Hallucination, explained by the very mechanism you just poked at.

Because the model is always producing its best guess at the next word — never checking a fact against a verified source — it can generate something that reads perfectly confidently and is simply not true. This is called a hallucination, and it's not a bug that gets fixed with a better prompt; it's a direct consequence of how prediction works.

Worth remembering

A hallucination doesn't raise its hand and say "I'm not totally sure about this one." It's delivered in the exact same fluent, confident tone as everything else the model produces — no hedging, no nervous throat-clearing — which is exactly why it's risky in a work context, and exactly why review can't be optional, no matter how convincing it sounds.

Try spotting one yourself. Below is a realistic Copilot-style answer to "Summarize this project's open issues" — asked with nothing attached. Click each sentence you think is a fabricated, ungrounded claim.

This project closed 6 open issues this week, down from 11 the week before. Since I wasn't given the actual issue export, I can't confirm current counts. The most critical one, issue #306, was root-caused to a race condition in the payment-retry handler. You may want to attach the latest issues.csv so I can check these numbers directly. Overall issue count improved by 18% compared to the previous three weeks.

What this means in practice, for any Copilot-assisted work:

  1. Never given, still answered
    Ask for an issue count you didn't attach data for, and the model may still produce a plausible-sounding number — because "I don't have that" is a less likely next output than a confident-sounding answer.
  2. Fluent ≠ verified
    An issue number, a root cause, or a percentage can be entirely fabricated while reading exactly like a real one — as you just saw above.
  3. The fix is human review, not a smarter prompt
    Treat every number, name, or fact in AI output as a claim to verify — not a finding to forward.

Five common myths

Click a card. Watch a myth quietly retire.

Myth

"It understands what I mean, the way a colleague would."

Click to reveal →
Reality

It recognizes patterns in your wording, full stop. Ambiguous phrasing doesn't make it pause and ask "wait, which one do you mean?" the way a colleague would — it just picks a guess and runs with it, confidently.

Myth

"More context always makes the answer better."

Click to reveal →
Reality

Relevant, targeted context beats sheer volume, every time. Dump in every file you own "just in case" and you'll get a longer answer, not a better one — plus more of it for you to re-read later.

Myth

"If it sounds confident, it's probably right."

Click to reveal →
Reality

Tone and accuracy have never actually met. As Chapter 3 showed, a fabricated fact reads exactly as smoothly as a correct one — confidence is not a fact-checking service.

Myth

"It remembers everything from our other conversations."

Click to reveal →
Reality

A new chat thread opens with a clean slate and zero memory of other threads, by default — no matter how memorable last Tuesday's conversation was. Anything it should "know" has to be re-attached or saved as a custom instruction — more on this in Chapter 5.

Myth

"Using it once teaches it our team's process forever."

Click to reveal →
Reality

One good chat doesn't retrain the model everyone else's Copilot runs on — it isn't secretly taking notes on your team. Consistency comes from saved instructions and files you provide each time, not from it quietly learning as you go.

05

Inside GitHub Copilot, today

How the tool is actually wired together — chat, agent mode, and everything it talks to.

GitHub Copilot Chat is the conversational front end to the model you just met. It shows up in more places than you'd expect for something with no legs: VS Code, github.com, GitHub Mobile, the command line, even JetBrains or Xcode editors — same underlying assistant, just wearing a different outfit each time.

Ask mode

You ask, it answers — drafts, explanations, summaries, one exchange at a time. Nothing happens behind your back: you stay in control of every step, and the reply in front of you is the whole story.

Plan mode

A middle ground between the two: you hand it a goal, and instead of acting right away, it proposes a step-by-step plan first. You review and adjust the plan itself before any actual work begins — sign-off up front, rather than watching or reviewing after the fact.

Agent mode

You hand it a goal in plain English and it takes the wheel: planning a multi-step task on its own, choosing tools as it goes, adapting based on what it finds — with far less hand-holding from you. This is the mode that can reach outside tools through MCP, described below.

Neither mode starts from nothing. Copilot fills in the gaps around your message from three places:

Custom instructions

A saved file — like copilot-instructions.md in a repository — that Copilot reads automatically, so you don't retype the same context every time. Personal instructions you set for yourself take priority over a repository's, which in turn take priority over an organization-wide one.

What you attach

Files and data you explicitly bring into the conversation. Without this, Copilot has nothing real to work from — the exact anti-pattern from Chapter 3.

MCP connections

In agent mode, MCP ("Model Context Protocol") lets Copilot reach live systems — such as a repository's own GitHub issues and pull requests — though the same pattern connects other trackers, docs tools, and testing systems too — scoped to your own existing permissions, never more.

Takeaway

Copilot isn't one fixed thing — it's a chat/agent interface sitting on top of an LLM, wired up to whatever instructions, files, and connected tools you give it. Better inputs are the entire lever you have for better outputs.

06

Intent → Prompt → Context → Output

The four-step chain behind every single reply you get — good or bad.

Every time Copilot Chat hands you something, four things happened in sequence, whether you noticed them or not. A good outcome isn't luck — it's making sure none of these four links is weak.

Intent

The actual goal in your head before you type a word. "I want a clean status update for tomorrow's stand-up" is an intent — nobody but you can see it yet.

Prompt

Your intent, translated into instructions. The model never sees your intent directly — only however well, or badly, your prompt captured it.

Context

Everything else the model can currently see while it answers: the conversation so far, files you've attached, saved custom instructions, and, in agent mode, whatever a live connection such as your repository's issues hands back through MCP.

Output / Outcome

What actually comes back — generated purely from Prompt + Context. A crystal-clear intent paired with a vague prompt and no context still produces a disappointing outcome. The model can't read your mind; it can only read what's in front of it.

Why this matters

Every prompt-engineering habit in this guide is really about closing the gap between Intent and Prompt, and making sure the right Context actually reaches the model. Get those two links right and the Output mostly takes care of itself.

So what exactly counts as "context"?

It's everything the model folds in alongside your prompt before it predicts a single word: every earlier message in this thread, any file or data you've attached, saved custom instructions (personal, repository, or organization-wide, from Chapter 5), and, in agent mode, whatever a connected tool such as your repository's issues returns through MCP. None of it is your prompt exactly, but all of it shapes the answer just as much as your prompt does.

Back to the rhyme, one more time

"Johnny Johnny" works as an instant prediction because the context — everything said just before it — makes "yes, papa" the obvious next line. Change the context and those same two words predict something else entirely: after "Doctor, doctor," the reply isn't "yes, papa," it's the setup for a joke. Same prediction mechanism, different context, different output. That's this whole chapter, in one rhyme.

The catch: there's a limit, called the context window.

A model can only hold so much text "in view" at once — that ceiling is its context window, usually measured in small chunks of text called tokens rather than whole words. Your prompt, every attached file, the saved instructions, and the entire back-and-forth of the conversation so far all have to fit inside that one window at the same time. It isn't unlimited, and it isn't memory in the human sense — it's closer to a whiteboard of a fixed size.

The whiteboard, filling up
Instructions
Attached files
Conversation so far
New message

All four have to share one fixed-size window. As "Conversation so far" keeps growing in a long-running thread, something eventually has to give — and what gives is the oldest content, pushed quietly out of view.

Once the window is full, the oldest parts of the conversation stop being visible to the model — not deleted from your screen, just no longer part of what it's reading when it predicts the next word. In practice, that shows up as the model seeming to "forget" something you said forty messages ago. It didn't forget in the human sense; it simply fell off the edge of the whiteboard.

Why this matters day to day

This is the real mechanism behind two habits from this guide: starting a fresh thread per task keeps the window lean and relevant instead of clogged with unrelated history, and being precise about exactly what you attach means the window fills up with what actually matters, not padding (both from Chapter 7).

07

Prompt engineering: the foundations

Five habits that turn a "meh" first draft into one you'd actually send — with three worked examples.

1 · General → specific

Open broad, then narrow — rather than trying to nail every requirement in one dense sentence.

2 · Give examples

Show the shape or tone you want. Examples anchor the model far more reliably than description alone.

3 · Break into steps

"Analyze, then summarize" outperforms one giant combined ask — and is easier to check along the way.

4 · Be precise

Name the exact file or data source you mean. Vague asks get vague, ungrounded answers.

5 · Iterate

The first response is a starting point. Point out what's missing or wrong, and ask again — refining beats restarting.

Back to the rhyme

In Chapter 2, "Yes, papa" came out instantly because "Johnny Johnny…" is such a strong, familiar pattern that there's really only one likely next line. A vague prompt is the opposite of that — it's like saying just "Johnny…" on its own: dozens of directions are equally plausible, so the model is left guessing much harder, and the reply drifts. Every habit above — examples, steps, precision — does one job: turn your prompt into something as unambiguous as "Johnny Johnny, yes papa," so the model's next-word guessing has one obvious direction instead of many.

Three real tasks, side by side — the version that gets a shrug, and the version that gets read:

Example A — Status report

Weak

"Summarize the sprint." Nothing attached — no file, no ask about format.

Applies the habits

"Using the attached sprint-export.csv, draft a 4-bullet status update: scope delivered, blockers, risks, next-sprint carryover."

Example B — Risk review from meeting notes

Weak

"Are there any risks in this project?" — no notes attached, no definition of "risk."

Applies the habits

"From the attached team-meeting-notes.md, list anything that could delay delivery — blocked items, scope changes, or dependencies mentioned. I'll judge which are real risks."

Example C — Backlog grooming prep

Weak

"Help me groom the backlog." Too broad to act on — grooming for what, exactly?

Applies the habits

"Using backlog.csv, first list items with no priority set, then separately list items untouched for 3+ sprints. Two short lists, issue numbers only."

One more habit

Start a fresh chat thread for each new, unrelated task. The model treats the whole conversation as context — so last week's PR drama can quietly gate-crash today's status report if you reuse the same thread (this is the myth from Chapter 4, back for an encore).

A simple prompt framework

Four slots. Fill them in order, every time, and stop starting from a blank cursor — try the builder below.

PartWhat it doesExample, for a retro summary
Role / ContextSets who you are and the situation."You're preparing a team retro."
Data ReferenceNames exactly what to use."Use the attached retro-notes.md."
Output FormatSpecifies the shape of the answer."Three sections: Went well, Didn't go well, Action items."
ConstraintsSets limits — length, tone, what to exclude."Keep each bullet under 20 words. No names, only teams."

Notice the shape: a fixed structure the model can lean on, the same way "Johnny Johnny" leaves no doubt that "yes, papa" comes next. Fill all four slots and you've given the model a pattern as clear as a rhyme it already knows — fill only one or two, and you're back to guessing.

09

Do's and don'ts, in practice

Five quick decisions — pick one, and find out if your instincts are trustworthy.

1. You need a summary of yesterday's stand-up notes. What do you do first?
2. Copilot gives you a defect count in its first response. What next?
3. You're about to ask about an unrelated new task, in the same thread you used yesterday for a different one. Best move?
4. A colleague asks you to paste a customer's proprietary requirements doc into Copilot Chat for a quick summary. What do you do?
5. The first draft Copilot gives you is close, but the tone is wrong and it's missing one section. What's the better next step?

Same habits, condensed into a checklist you can actually glance at mid-sprint:

Do

  • Attach the real file or data before asking for anything about it.
  • Name the exact source, format, and length you want.
  • Treat every response as a first draft, not a finished answer.
  • Verify any number, name, or fact before it goes in a report.
  • Start a new thread for each new, unrelated task.
  • Iterate — tell it what was wrong, then ask again.

Don't

  • Ask it to "summarize" or "analyze" with nothing attached.
  • Treat its output as a verified source of truth.
  • Assume a confident tone means a correct answer.
  • Let it replace your judgment on a decision.
  • Paste sensitive or proprietary data without checking policy first.
  • Reuse one long-running thread across unrelated tasks.

Worked walkthrough: building one prompt, start to finish

Everything in this guide, applied to one real, very Friday-afternoon task.

The situation: It's Friday afternoon. Leadership wants a risk review by Monday morning. Your meeting notes are a mess and your weekend plans do not involve a backlog export. Let's get you out of here on time.

Step 1 · Recognize the good fit (Ch. 1)

Spotting risks across notes and backlog data is pattern-spotting across text you provide — a good fit. Deciding whether a risk is serious enough to escalate stays your call, not the model's.

Step 2 · Plan for verification, not blind trust (Ch. 3)

Before you even open the chat, decide: any specific issue numbers, owners, or dates it surfaces get checked against the actual backlog before they reach the leadership deck.

Step 3 · Open a fresh thread (Ch. 4 & 7)

This is a new task from whatever you worked on earlier this week — new thread, so nothing from an unrelated conversation leaks in.

Step 4 · Build the prompt with the framework (Ch. 8)
Assembled from Role/Context + Data Reference + Output Format + ConstraintsYou're preparing for a leadership risk review. Using the attached backlog.csv and team-meeting-notes.md, list anything that could delay delivery — blocked items, scope changes, or dependencies mentioned in the notes. Group into three sections: Blocked, At risk, Watching. Issue numbers and one line each, no more than 8 items total.
Step 5 · Review before it goes anywhere (Ch. 3 & 9)

Check every issue number and claim against the real backlog. Decide which flagged items are genuine risks — that judgment call stays yours. Then it's ready for Monday, and so, mercifully, are you.
